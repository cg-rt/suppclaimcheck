How a check works

The language model reads; code decides. A model labels passages and copies sentences out of articles. Code checks every sentence against the article, then works out the verdict and the score.

This page describes the method as it runs now: {{ m.pipeline }} with {{ m.rubric }}. Every result names the version it ran on.

In one paragraph

SuppClaimCheck searches PubMed for open-access studies about the claim, takes the strongest kinds of study first, and reads up to {{ m.read }} of them. A language model labels passages as supporting the claim, contradicting it, or not about it, and copies a sentence as proof. Code checks every copied sentence word for word against the article and throws away any that don't match. Code then weighs each article by its type of study and works out the verdict and a score from 0 to 100. The model never decides the verdict or the score.

Where the claim comes from

  • You paste a text, or give a link to a page: an article, a blog post or a product page.
  • For a link, the page reader fetches that one page, if the site allows it, and keeps its readable text. The model looks for claims in the first {{ "{:,}".format(m.page_chars) }} characters and lists them, the most prominent first. What the page reader does.
  • You confirm one claim, and can correct it, before anything is searched or spent.
  • Some requests are refused or answered with a question instead, such as personal dosing advice or several claims at once. Claims about pregnancy, children, serious illness, very high doses or medicines get a safety notice above the result.

Finding articles

  • Code builds a PubMed search from the supplement, the effect and the population you confirmed.
  • The search also tries the supplement's other names (ashwagandha and Withania somnifera) and other spellings (anaemia and anemia), because PubMed matches a word in a title or abstract only as written.
  • Up to {{ m.considered }} matches are considered. Code sorts them by type of study, strongest first, and PubMed's own order breaks ties. The first {{ m.read }} that can be read are read in full.
  • Only open-access articles whose licence allows text mining can be read. The others are listed under the result as found, not read.
  • Supplements are taken by mouth, so a study whose title says the substance was injected or given by drip is listed as not read, unless your claim is about injections.
  • If the same claim was checked in the last {{ m.reuse_days }} days on the same method version, that result is shown again, with a note saying so.

Types of study and their weight

Code reads each article's publication types and subject headings in PubMed and puts it in one group. An article too new to have headings is placed by a few words in its title, such as “rats” or “patients”, and failing that it is “type unknown”.

The weights are versioned settings, not fixed rules: changing one means running the evaluation set again. Sample size isn't used, because PubMed's records don't give it.

Reading each article

  • Each article is split into passages. The {{ m.passages }} that best match the claim go to the model, one article at a time. “Best match” blends two rankings: passages that share the claim's words (weight {{ "%.1f"|format(m.keyword_weight) }}) and passages close to it in meaning, from a small model that runs on SuppClaimCheck's own server (weight {{ "%.1f"|format(1 - m.keyword_weight) }}).
  • For each passage the model gives a label (supports, contradicts or not about the claim), an exact quote and a one-sentence reason. A finding that the supplement does the opposite of the claim counts as contradicting it.
  • Code checks every quote against the passage it came from. Only typesetting may differ: line breaks, dashes, curly quotation marks and letters set as one character (ligatures). One changed, added or missing word, a changed number or a different capital letter fails the check. A quote that fails is dropped and logged. It never counts and is never shown.

From quotes to a verdict

Each article counts once, however many quotes it has. Its stance is its quotes for the claim minus its quotes against, divided by all its verified quotes: +1 if they all agree with the claim, −1 if they all disagree. An article without a verified quote has no finding and doesn't count.

Direction
The stances, averaged by weight: from −1, all against, to +1, all for.
Strength
The total weight of the articles with a finding, divided by {{ "%.1f"|format(m.full_strength) }}, at most 1. Two systematic reviews give full strength.
Score
50 + 50 × direction × strength, rounded. 0 is consistent strong evidence against, 50 balanced or too weak to call, 100 consistent strong evidence for.

The verdict is the first rule that applies

  1. No article could be read. Not found
  2. No article has a verified finding. Insufficient evidence
  3. The only findings come from animal, lab or unknown types of study. Insufficient evidence
  4. Strength is below {{ "%.2f"|format(m.min_strength) }}. Insufficient evidence
  5. The smaller side holds at least {{ pct.format(m.mixed_share) }} of the weighted evidence. Mixed
  6. Otherwise, by the direction. Supported or Contradicted

  3. Strength: {{ "%.2f"|format(example.total) }} ÷ {{ "%.2f"|format(m.full_strength) }}, at most 1, is {{ "%.2f"|format(example.strength) }}
  4. Score: 50 + 50 × {{ "%+.2f"|format(example.direction) }} × {{ "%.2f"|format(example.strength) }} = {{ example.score }}

What it can't do

  • It reads only open-access articles, and at most {{ m.read }} per claim. Strong evidence behind a paywall is missed.
  • Labelling passages is the model's judgement and can be wrong. Quotes are always verified; labels are not. An evaluation set measures how often the verdict is right, and every change to the method is run against it.
  • The type of study comes from PubMed's records, which can be missing or coarse. “Human study, design not stated” can be anything from a trial to a survey.
  • A linked page is read as it is at that moment, from the top: a claim near the end of a long page can be missed. Pages that need a login or JavaScript, and PDFs, can't be read.
  • A verdict describes what the articles read say about the claim as you confirmed it. It isn't medical advice, and it says nothing about whether a dose is right or safe for you.
